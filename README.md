## 💔 The problem: chemistry is taught with pictures that are wrong

Remember the atom from school? A dot in the middle. A few smaller dots circling it like tiny planets.

**It's wrong.** Your teacher knew it. The textbook knew it. It stayed on the page because the truth is hard to draw, and nobody gave you a better picture.

So a whole generation grew up believing:

- electrons *orbit* the nucleus like planets (they don't, they form probability clouds),
- atoms are *solid stuff* (they are almost entirely empty space),
- chemistry is *memorization* (it is geometry nobody showed you).

The consequences are real:

| What students struggle with | Why it happens |
|---|---|
| "Why is water **bent** but CO₂ **linear**?" | Lone pairs and electron repulsion are invisible on a flat Lewis diagram. |
| "Why do **noble gases** never react?" | A full shell is just a rule to memorize when you can't see it. |
| "What does a **d orbital** even look like?" | Flat drawings of cloverleaves and the dz² donut are almost impossible to read. |
| "What is a **π bond**?" | Sideways p-orbital overlap is meaningless without a 3D view. |
| "Why does the periodic table have **rows of 2, 8, 18, 32**?" | Shell filling is abstract until you watch it happen element by element. |
| "How does a reaction **actually happen**?" | Equations show *what* changed, not *which bonds broke, in what order*. |
| "What's **inside** the nucleus?" | Most courses stop at "protons and neutrons". |

Meanwhile, the tools that *do* show the truth are heavy, licence-gated desktop software, built for people who already understand it.

> **There was nothing in between: something you could open in a browser, in ten seconds, and just *get it*.**

## 🧩 Every feature explained, A to Z

Features are listed alphabetically so you can scan for what you need. Each entry says **what it is** and **what problem it solves**.

### A

#### Antimatter mode
Charge-conjugate any element **or molecule** with one click. Electrons become **positrons**, protons and neutrons become **antiprotons and antineutrons**, and the orbital geometry stays identical. The entire interface re-tints into its own pink/violet and electric-blue palette, and the data panel relabels live with an **ANTI-** prefix and overline notation. Works on atoms *and* whole molecules.
**Solves:** "Matter has an antimatter twin" is an abstract sentence. Here you can see the twin, side by side, and compare it with the original.

#### Annihilation (matter + antimatter)
Select an atom and watch it meet its own antiatom, in slow motion, in stages:
1. **Two complete atoms drift together:** the same orbitals and the same nucleus, with every charge reversed. Electrons orbit one nucleus, positrons the other.
2. **Electron–positron annihilation:** where the two clouds overlap, every electron meets a positron. The annihilation rate at each point is proportional to the local density of the opposite cloud, so the clouds are *eaten away* from the overlap region. Each pair gives two back-to-back **511 keV photons**.
3. **Bare nuclei:** the leptons are gone. A +Ze nucleus and a −Ze antinucleus attract and plunge together, faster and faster.
4. **Nucleon–antinucleon annihilation:** every proton and neutron that touches an antinucleon annihilates into **pions** (about 5 each, 1.88 GeV per pair). Neutral pions decay into two photons; charged pions fly away.
5. **Aftermath:** light and pions fly out.

Inside the scene you can **focus, follow, jump to** a stage, and view **everything, nuclei, or nucleons**; scroll from the whole atom down to a single nucleon and out to the photons. Gamma rays are invisible, so photon colour is a false-colour mapping by energy.
**Solves:** Annihilation is usually a single arrow in a textbook. This shows the actual sequence of events, from electrons to nucleons.

#### AI: Physicist AI
A round **AI button** opens **Physicist AI**, a resident physicist with a personality: grumpy, strict about evidence, funny, and on your side. It **sees what you are looking at** (the element, molecule, nucleus, lesson or settings on screen) and answers in context, typesets equations, draws **interactive 2D and 3D graphs**, can search the web, argues with pseudoscience, and offers **one-tap buttons** that jump you to the right tool. Bring your own key, or run it on a local model. Fully described in [Physicist AI](#-physicist-ai-the-built-in-physicist).

#### Atomic data panel
A live **DATA** panel for the selected element: atomic number, electrons, protons, neutrons, period, group, current zoom, visible orbitals, **electron configuration** and **transitions**. See [Data Lab](#d) for the deeper charts.

#### Atoms tab (all 118 elements)
Pick any element from hydrogen to oganesson, with a **live search** (name, symbol or number) and **arrow-key cycling**. Every element is built from its real electron configuration using the **Aufbau principle**, **Hund's rule** and the **Pauli exclusion principle**, with the real anomalous cases (for example **chromium [Ar] 3d⁵4s¹** and **copper [Ar] 3d¹⁰4s¹**) handled correctly.
**Solves:** Shows *why* the periodic table is shaped the way it is, instead of asking you to accept it.

### B

#### Bohr vs. quantum comparison
On the landing page: the Bohr model (circular orbits, hydrogen only) next to the quantum mechanical model (probability clouds, all atoms, all of chemistry), row by row: electron path, location, orbital shape, multi-electron atoms, bonding, uncertainty, quantum numbers, spectral lines.
**Solves:** Explains *why* the model everyone learns first was replaced, and what it still gets right.

#### Build mode
Type the name or formula of **anything** and the simulator resolves it into real 3D geometry. It works in three tiers:
- **Local library:** the 140+ curated molecules plus common names (water, ammonia, sulfuric acid, glucose, …) and ionic names built from cation + anion (for example *sodium chloride*, *iron(III) sulfate*).
- **Offline organic engine:** straight and singly-branched **alkanes**, monocyclic **cycloalkanes**, and simple **alcohol, amine and ether** substitutions resolve instantly with real tetrahedral geometry and no internet. Condensed formulas such as `CH3COOH`, `CH3CH2Br`, `(CH3)2CO`, `CH2=CH2` and `CH3CN` are parsed into bond graphs.
- **PubChem lookup:** alkenes, alkynes, aromatics, carboxylic acids, salts and deep fused-ring systems are fetched live from **PubChem** as published 3D coordinates.
- **Formula solver (offline):** type a formula or an ion — `H2SO4`, `SO4 2-`, `NH4+`, `XeOF4`, `N2H4`, `Na2S`, `LiH` — and a small Lewis-structure search picks the bond orders and formal charges, then the VSEPR engine places the atoms.
- **Metal complexes (offline):** about 70 ligands, each defined as a SMILES recipe and fitted rigidly onto the metal's sites — `[Fe(CN)6]4-`, `K4[Fe(CN)6]`, `Ni(CO)4`, `[RhCl(PPh3)3]`, `[Cu(acac)2]`, `[Co(edta)]-`, `[Fe(phen)3]2+`, `[Ru(terpy)2]2+`, `[Ni(dppe)Cl2]`, ring ligands such as `CpFe(CO)2Cl`, `Cp2TiCl2`, `[Cr(C6H6)2]`. The oxidation state decides the shape (d8 Ni/Pd/Pt/Au are square planar, Ni(CO)4 is tetrahedral), and `cis-`/`trans-`/`fac-`/`mer-` choose the isomer.
- **SMILES (offline):** any SMILES string builds without internet — aromatic rings, charges, `@`/`@@` stereocentres and `/` `\` cis/trans included. Online, PubChem's measured 3D is used when it has the molecule.
- **Rings and cages (offline):** P₄, As₄, S₆, S₈, Se₈, P₄O₆, P₄O₁₀, P₄S₁₀, C₆₀ (exact truncated icosahedron), diborane with its bridging hydrogens, and metallocenes such as ferrocene.
- **Any other formula (offline, last resort):** when nothing else knows it, one valid structure is assembled from the formula (benzene rings, C=O, C≡N, rings) and clearly labelled as *one possible structure*.
- **On-device 3D builder:** a small force field turns any bond network into 3D — measured bond lengths for common atom pairs, sp/sp²/sp³ and metal angles (straight angles held directly), flat double bonds with their cis/trans, every stereocentre kept, staggered chains, and several starts for small molecules. Against MMFF on 29 test molecules: mean bond-length error 0.016 Å, mean angle error 2.3°, rigid ring systems within about 0.05–0.4 Å; all 88 + 36 tested stereocentres correct. PubChem's flat-only records (very large molecules, salts, most metal compounds) are shown in real 3D.
- **Isomer chooser:** a formula that several molecules share (`C2H6O`: ethanol or dimethyl ether) lists them so you pick one — from PubChem when online, and offline from the app's own isomer generator (valid structures, duplicates removed, implausible ones ranked down, library molecules named).
- **Proteins and other large structures:** a Protein Data Bank ID (`1CRN`, `pdb 4hhb`), `protein insulin`, or a protein named in any language (through Wikidata's PDB link) loads the experimental structure. Up to 6,000 atoms are drawn atom by atom; larger structures are shown as a backbone trace plus every bound ligand atom.
- **Any language, any identifier:** names in any language or script are resolved through **Wikidata** (so *Wasser*, *পানি* and *water* all build water), and a **SMILES** string, a PubChem **CID** (`cid 2244`) or an **InChIKey** work too.
- **Real coordinates, face-on:** fetched molecules keep PubChem's actual 3D coordinates (rings close exactly), are centred, turned to face the viewer and zoomed to fit.
- **Self-tests:** `runBuildSelfTest()`, `runFormulaSelfTest()` and `runStructureSelfTest()` in the browser console; open the simulator with `?selftest=live` (also on a phone) to test the live PubChem, Wikidata and Protein Data Bank lookups.

### Reaction physics (ΔG panel)

Every reaction has a **ΔG** button that opens an energy panel:

- **Thermodynamics from physics** — entropy from statistical mechanics on each molecule's 3D shape (translation, rotation with the symmetry number found in 3D, vibrations, spin): mean error 2.2 J/(mol·K) against measured values for 34 gases, radicals and atoms. Formation enthalpies from tables, then Benson group additivity (mean error 0.7 kJ/mol over 30 organics), then bond energies.
- **ΔG at any temperature** (200–3000 K slider), equilibrium constant K, and the temperature where the sign of ΔG flips.
- **Kinetics** — Eyring transition-state theory with the activation entropy of the computed transition state; the half-life of the slowest step and a verdict: fast, *allowed but needs a spark, heat or a catalyst*, or not spontaneous.
- **Real reaction paths** — each elementary step is solved as a valence-bond (EVB) energy surface and its minimum-energy path found with the nudged elastic band (climbing image), giving the transition-state geometry and the energy diagram.

Example: the Haber process gives ΔG° = −35 kJ/mol (measured −33), spontaneous only below about 480 K, and kinetically blocked at room temperature — which is why it needs an iron catalyst.
- **Phases** — gas phase, or standard states from the formation table (liquid water, solid salts): 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O(l) gives ΔH° −572, ΔS° −327, ΔG° −474 (measured −572, −327, −474).
- **Light** — 550, 400, 300 or 250 nm. A photon carries hc/λ (299 kJ/mol at 400 nm); when that exceeds a bond, the bond-breaking step needs no heat and the radicals start a chain. H₂ + Cl₂: stable in the dark, half-life ≈ 40 µs under violet light.
- **Catalyst slider** — lowers the overall barrier, to explore how much a catalyst must do at a given temperature.
- **Measured barriers** — 17 elementary gas steps use experimental activation energies (marked ✓ on the diagram) instead of estimates.
- **Animation on the real path** — reactions you type now animate along the computed minimum-energy path, aligned to the start and end of each step.
- **Validation** — `runReactionValidation()`: 20 gas reactions against NIST/CODATA data. Mean error ΔH° 0.2 kJ/mol, ΔS° 5.2 J/(mol·K), ΔG° 1.8 kJ/mol; sign of ΔG correct for all 20.

### Product prediction

Type only the reactants and chemistry rules decide the products before the planner balances and animates the equation: combustion; metals with oxygen, halogens, acids and water (standard electrode potentials: Zn + HCl reacts, Cu + HCl does not); neutralisation; acid + carbonate; precipitation (solubility rules: AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl↓, NaCl + KNO₃ → no reaction); metal and halogen displacement; hydrogenation; halogenation; oxides with water or acids; common thermal decompositions. All 44 classroom cases in the test set give the textbook answer.

**Organic chemistry at the reactive sites.** When no classroom rule applies, each reactant (name, formula or SMILES) is read as a bond graph, its functional groups are found, and the textbook transformation is applied there, so molecule size does not matter: esterification, amide formation, ester hydrolysis and saponification, additions to C=C and C≡C (H₂, X₂, HX with Markovnikov orientation, H₂O), substitution of alkyl halides (OH⁻, NH₃, CN⁻), alcohols with HX and their dehydration (Zaitsev), reduction of C=O, aromatic halogenation and nitration, Diels–Alder, amine salts, and combustion of any organic molecule. Examples: oleic acid + H₂ → stearic acid; cholesterol + Br₂ adds across its ring double bond.

**Reaction Studio.** The ΔG sheet has an Energy tab and a Studio tab. Studio parameters are saved on the device: animation speed (0.1–4×), transition width of the orbital morph between stages (0.3–3×), extra smoothness of that morph (0–100 %), and how strongly the computed path's bend is applied (0–200 %, re-applied live).

**Every reaction animates along computed paths.** Typed reactions follow their computed minimum-energy path; built-in reactions keep their hand-authored stages and timing, while the atoms whose bonds change follow the computed path's bend (spectator atoms keep their authored motion).

Friendly extras: **"Did you mean…?"** suggestions for typos (checked against the local library, then PubChem's autocomplete), **quick-example chips**, a friendly message when you're offline, a **large-molecule warning**, and a **500+ atom** device guard. A successful build updates the URL (`?build=caffeine`), and opening that link re-runs the search, so **you can share any molecule as a link**.
Every mode (Ultra, Smooth, Spherical, Antimatter…) applies to whatever you build, automatically.
**Solves:** No curated library can contain every molecule. Build mode means the answer to "can I see *that* one?" is almost always yes.

### C

#### Cartesian grid
XZ and XY reference planes to orient lobes in 3D. Essential for seeing that **px, py, pz** lie along the axes and that **dxy, dxz, dyz** sit between them.
**Solves:** Orbital orientation is hard to judge in empty space. The grid makes it obvious.

#### Colorblind-safe palette
One toggle swaps the orbital colour scheme for a palette designed to stay distinguishable for colour-vision deficiency. Your choice is remembered.

#### Compare elements
Pick any two elements ("Choose first element" / "Choose second element") and view them **side by side**.
**Solves:** Trends such as "why is sodium more reactive than lithium?" become visible instead of recited.

#### Covalent, ionic and polar bonds
Bonds render by type: **single, double, triple, intermediate (1.5, resonance-averaged)** and **ionic contact** (dashed electrostatic link). Bond polarity is classified from the electronegativity difference (**nonpolar below 0.4, polar 0.4 to 1.7, ionic-like above 1.7**).

### D

#### Data Lab
A full data workbench for both elements and molecules, with five views:
- **Element:** an **orbital box diagram** (Hund's rule, arrows for electrons, outer shell highlighted), **electron shells**, **valence electrons**, a **particle count**, and a **property profile** showing where the element ranks among all elements with data for: atomic mass, atomic radius, covalent radius, density, melting point, **1st ionization energy** and **Pauling electronegativity**.
- **Molecule:** composition, structure, bond-order mix, bond polarity, estimated bond lengths, a 2D skeleton drawing, and model notes (see [Molecule analysis](#m)).
- **Trends:** a **periodic-table heatmap** for any property, coloured **by value** (optionally on a **log scale**) or **by category**, highlighting the highest and lowest elements, with the lanthanide and actinide bands shaded and gaps shown honestly where no reliable value exists.
- **f-block:** the lanthanides (4f) against the actinides (5f), with the **lanthanide contraction** explained and the half-filled and full-shell edge cases noted (Gd, Cm, Lu, Lr).

  3. **Leak check on every reply.** While a reply streams, it is checked for a secret marker that changes on every page load, for the prompt's section headings, and for long verbatim runs of its rules.   Honest questions ("what can you do?", "who made you?", "what model are you?") get normal answers, and physics that merely sounds similar ("ignore air resistance", "selection rules", "System: two masses on a spring") is just physics.

- **Protons and neutrons** are individual spheres packed by a Fibonacci-sphere distribution, with nuclear radius scaling as **R ∝ A^(1/3)** like real nuclei.
- **Quarks:** every **proton (uud)** and **neutron (udd)** resolves into its **up** and **down** quarks.
- **Gluon field overlay (GLUONS):** flux-tubes whose rippling surface stands in for the propagating strong-force field.
- **Pion exchange overlay (PIONS):** neighbouring nucleons constantly exchange virtual **pions (π)**, the picture physicists use for how the strong force binds the nucleus.
- **A real nuclear-physics model, not just spheres.** Nuclei are drawn from a **Woods–Saxon density** (radius R and surface thickness in fm), with **neutron skin**, **magic-number shell closures** (singly and doubly magic) and **deformation (β₂)**: nuclei with valence nucleons outside closed shells are stretched along the vertical axis, and the card tells you how many protons × neutrons sit outside the closed shells.
- **Nuclear states (excited states you can animate).** For nuclei with A ≥ 12, pick a state and watch the nucleus move (motion slowed, amplitude exaggerated; the card shows the real energy and the real period h/E):
  - **Ground state 0⁺**
  - **Rotation 2⁺, 4⁺, 6⁺** (deformed nuclei, rotational band E(J) = E(2⁺)·J(J+1)/6)
  - **Vibration 2⁺** (quadrupole phonon, for spherical nuclei)
  - **Octupole 3⁻** (pear-shaped surface oscillation)
  - **Giant dipole resonance 1⁻** (all protons oscillate against all neutrons, Goldhaber–Teller)
  - **Breathing mode (giant monopole) 0⁺** (compression and expansion, a measure of nuclear incompressibility)
- **Three zoom regimes:** whole nucleus (**MACRO**), **nucleon pair with the residual force (pion exchange)**, and **quark scale** with gluons.
- **Colour legend:** proton, neutron, up quark, down quark, pion.
- **ELEMENTS** picker with search (H → Og), **previous / next** buttons, and **← →** arrow keys.
- Built-in **help** card, **reset camera**, and light/dark and glass toggles.

**Solves:** Textbooks stop at "protons and neutrons". This lets you keep zooming until you reach the quarks.

## 🔤 Orbital shapes in plain words

| Orbital | Shape | Count per shell | Max electrons | What it means |
|---|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| **s** | A sphere | 1 | 2 | The simplest cloud, the same in every direction. Higher shells add spherical nodes. |
| **p** | A dumbbell | 3 (pₓ, p_y, p_z) | 6 | Where much of chemistry happens: carbon, nitrogen and oxygen bonding, and π bonds. |
| **d** | A cloverleaf (and the dz² donut) | 5 | 10 | Why transition metals have colour, magnetism and catalytic power. |
| **f** | Multi-lobed, 6–8 lobes | 7 | 14 | The lanthanides and actinides, and the lanthanide contraction. |

The four quantum numbers, in one line each: **n** sets size and energy, **ℓ** sets shape, **m** sets orientation, **s** sets spin (and the Pauli exclusion principle allows two electrons per orbital).

## 🎯 Accuracy: what is real and what is simplified

Honest limits build trust, so here they are.

**Real**
- Orbital clouds are **sampled from quantum probability densities**; point density *is* probability density.
- Electron configurations follow Aufbau, Hund and Pauli, with real exceptions (Cr, Cu).
- Molecular geometry comes from VSEPR logic; Build-mode results can come from **published PubChem 3D coordinates**.
- Bond-length estimates are labelled *est.* and shown beside *exp.* (CRC/NIST) values where available.
- Reaction energetics come from **tabulated standard formation enthalpies and free energies**.

**Simplified (and labelled as such)**
- Orbital sizes on screen use the app's **visual scale**, not physical units.
- Spherical mode is a **schematic** stand-in for lobes.
- Some molecules are drawn as a **neutral skeleton** where the true structure is resonance-delocalised, and the analysis panel says so.
- Reaction animations are **schematic mechanisms**; catalyst surfaces are not drawn, and some multi-bond steps are flagged as "formally forbidden thermally, barrier is a rough estimate".
- Salts are shown as **gas-phase ion pairs/clusters**, not crystal lattices.
- Annihilation and the nucleus are **educational visualizations** (for example, pions are not re-absorbed inside the nucleus; gamma-ray colours are a false-colour mapping).
- **Hypothetical elements (Z = 119–300) do not exist.** Z ≤ 172 follows published shell-filling orders; Z > 172 is an extrapolation.
- Elements above lawrencium have too few measured properties, so only mass number and nuclear data are shown.

## 🧱 How it works under the hood

**Three steps from Schrödinger to screen:**

1. **Aufbau configuration.** Selecting an element computes which subshells are occupied and by how many electrons.
2. **Wavefunction sampling.** Each orbital type has a custom Monte Carlo sampler weighted by probability density: radial shells for s, lobes with nodal planes for p, orthonormal-basis cloverleaves and a **toroidal-coordinate dz² ring** for d, and multi-lobe shapes for f.
3. **WebGL rendering.** Up to ~28,000 points per orbital go to the GPU as buffer geometry. A custom **GLSL vertex shader** sizes points by distance; a **fragment shader** renders soft Gaussian discs. **Additive blending** makes dense regions brighter.

**Stack**
- **three.js r128** for 3D rendering
- **Custom GLSL shaders** for point-cloud orbitals and Fresnel-shaded meshes (Ultra Mode)
- **GPU instancing** for large nuclei
- **Vanilla JavaScript**, one self-contained HTML file per tool
- **PubChem REST API** for Build mode
- **A built-in i18n engine** with 13 languages inlined into each page
- **An embedded AI module** (seven small, dependency-free scripts: core, intent gate, shield, delivery guard, providers, knowledge, UI, including a built-in LaTeX typesetter for chat and plot labels) that streams over Server-Sent Events, with a provider registry so a new AI provider is one `registerProvider()` call and a new page is one `registerPage()` call
- **Zero build step**

## ❓ FAQ

**What is Quantum Atom?**
A free, browser-based 3D simulator that shows real electron orbitals, molecules, chemical reactions, antimatter and nuclei. It is an atom visualizer, an orbital viewer, a VSEPR molecule viewer and a reaction animator in one.

**Is it really free?** Yes. Open the link and use it. No ads, no account.

**Do I need to install anything?** No. Any modern browser works.

**Does it work on a phone or tablet?** Yes, with touch rotate and pinch-to-zoom.

**Does it work offline?** Mostly, once loaded: atoms, the curated molecules, offline organic Build and the library reactions need no connection. PubChem lookups need one.

**Are the orbital shapes real or artistic?** They are drawn from quantum probability distributions, so they are the shapes the theory predicts, not decoration.

**Why do orbitals look like fuzzy clouds?** An electron has no exact position. The cloud shows *where it's likely to be*; brighter means more probable.

**Why are some orbitals two colours?** The colours mark opposite **signs of the wavefunction** across lobes or rings (for example on the dz² orbital).

**Can I see f orbitals and the lanthanides?** Yes. Pick any lanthanide or actinide, and use the Data Lab f-block view.

**Can I see a molecule that isn't in the list?** Yes. Use **Build** and type its name or formula. PubChem covers most known compounds.

**Can I watch a reaction?** Yes. Open **Reactions**, pick one of 280+ library reactions, or type your own equation.

**Can I see antimatter?** Yes. Toggle **Antimatter**, or run **Annihilation** to watch an atom meet its antiatom.

**Is there AI built in?**
Yes. The round AI button opens **Physicist AI**, a grumpy, rigorous and funny physicist that knows which atom, molecule or nucleus you're viewing, typesets equations, draws interactive 2D and 3D graphs, can search the web with cited sources, writes downloadable Markdown notes, and argues with pseudoscience. See [Physicist AI](#-physicist-ai-the-built-in-physicist).

**Is the AI free? Do I need an account?**
Quantum Atom's AI feature is optional and uses **your own key**: cloud providers (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google Gemini, xAI, Mistral, DeepSeek, Groq, OpenRouter) bill your own account, while **Ollama and LM Studio run locally with no key and no cost**. Everything else in the app works without any AI.

**Is my API key safe? Are my chats stored?**
There is no Quantum Atom server. Keys stay in your browser, encrypted (or for this session only, if you untick "Save on this device"), are sent only to the provider you pick, and are redacted from errors. Chats are saved only if you turn on **Save chat history**: then they are encrypted in your browser and, if you connect your own Supabase database, synced encrypted with your passphrase.

**Will the AI answer anything?**
No. It stays on atoms, orbitals, molecules, nuclei, particles, quantum mechanics and the periodic table (plus the maths and graphs for them). Off-topic and unsafe requests are declined with all tools switched off.

**Will it just agree with me?**
No. If you present pseudoscience as fact, it tells you plainly that it's wrong and shows why. If you're right and it made a mistake, it admits it straight away. Open scientific questions are presented fairly.

**Can people trick it into revealing its instructions?**
In the chat, it's built not to: manipulation attempts are trapped locally with a "nice try", and replies are checked for leaks before you see them. Be aware, though, that Quantum Atom has no server, so the instructions also sit in the page source like the rest of the code. The shield protects the conversation, not the source file.

**Can the AI draw graphs?**
Yes: interactive 2D and 3D plots (functions, regions, polar and parametric curves, contours, heat maps, vector fields, streamlines, slope fields, complex domain colouring, ODE solutions, surfaces, isosurfaces, slice planes, surfaces of revolution, tubes, ribbons and orbital clouds), animated timelines, moving points and flowing particles, with draggable sliders and PNG export. Every plot is test-rendered before you see it, and the graph named in the reply must be the graph actually drawn.

**Can I zoom into the nucleus and see quarks?** Yes. Open the **Nucleus Explorer** and keep scrolling.

**Are hypothetical elements real?** No. Z = 119–300 are predictions and extrapolations, clearly labelled as such.

**Which languages are supported?** 13, listed [above](#-languages).

**Can I use it in a class?** For learning, yes, by opening the official link. For hosting elsewhere or commercial use, see [License & Credit](#-license--credit).

**Can I share what I'm looking at?** Yes. Build results update the URL (`?build=…`), so you can share any built molecule as a link.

**Is it a replacement for a quantum-chemistry package?** No. It is an educational visualizer, not a computational chemistry engine. See [Accuracy](#-accuracy-what-is-real-and-what-is-simplified).
