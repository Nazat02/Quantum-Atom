THE REAL SHAPE OF MATTER

Electrons aren't dots
on a track.

An electron doesn't circle the nucleus like a planet — it exists as a smeared-out wave, a cloud of probability with a genuine 3D shape. That shape depends on the orbital: 1s is a sphere, 2p is a dumbbell, 3d branches into four lobes. The cloud above is drawn from that real math, not from an artist's guess.

HOW TO READ AN ORBITAL

Four numbers describe
every electron.

Every cloud in the simulator is one solution of the Schrödinger equation, and each solution is labelled by four quantum numbers. Learn to read them and the periodic table stops being a list to memorise.

n

Principal · size & energy

n = 1, 2, 3 and up. The larger n is, the farther out the cloud sits and the more energy the electron has.

l

Angular · shape

l runs from 0 to n − 1. l = 0 is an s orbital, 1 is p, 2 is d, 3 is f.

mₗ

Magnetic · direction

Which way the orbital points. There are 2l + 1 choices: one s, three p, five d, seven f.

mₛ

Spin · up or down

Every electron has spin +½ or −½. Two electrons can share an orbital only if their spins are opposite.

RULE 1

Aufbau · lowest first

Electrons fill the lowest-energy orbitals first: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d and on. A few elements, like chromium and copper, bend the order.

RULE 2

Pauli · two per orbital

No two electrons share all four quantum numbers. That caps each orbital at two electrons, and each shell n at 2n²: 2, 8, 18, 32.

RULE 3

Hund · spread out first

Within a subshell, electrons occupy separate orbitals with parallel spins before they pair up, like passengers taking empty bus seats.

    HOW SMALL IS SMALL

    Eight orders of magnitude,
    atom to quark.

    Each step down in this scale is ten times smaller than the last. From the edge of the atom to the smallest thing anyone has measured, you cross eight of them.

    10⁻¹⁰ m

    The atom

    About ten million atoms fit across one millimetre. Nearly all of an atom's volume is the electron cloud.

    10⁻¹⁴ m

    The nucleus

    About 100,000 times smaller. Scale the atom to a 100 m stadium and the nucleus is a grain of sand at the centre, holding over 99.9% of the mass.

    10⁻¹⁵ m

    Proton & neutron

    Each is roughly 1.7 femtometres across, and is itself a swarm of quarks and gluons.

    <10⁻¹⁸ m

    Quark & electron

    No experiment has found any size for them. Their radius is below 10⁻¹⁸ m, so far as anyone can tell they are points.

    Masses are in MeV/c². A free neutron is unstable: it decays in about 15 minutes. Inside a nucleus it can be stable.

    QUESTIONS

    Good questions,
    short answers.

    Why a cloud instead of orbits?

    Quantum mechanics describes an electron with a wave function. Squaring it gives the probability of finding the electron at each point, so the cloud is denser where the electron is more likely to be. There is no path to draw, only odds.

    Is this what an atom really looks like?

    No picture is literal, because atoms are far smaller than the wavelength of visible light. The clouds are computed from the orbital mathematics, the same shapes chemistry textbooks show, and drawn as points of light so you can see their form.

    What does Real Image Mode do?

    It switches to a photographic-style rendering and lets you choose how the cloud is coloured: Ember, Brand, Warm, Red, Classic, Radial, Angular or Shell.

    What does Antimatter mode show?

    It swaps the scene to a mirror palette. In the Nucleus Explorer, protons and neutrons are relabelled as antiprotons and antineutrons.

    Are the electron configurations correct?

    They are ground-state configurations using the Madelung filling order, with the known exceptions (chromium, copper, palladium, gold and several lanthanides and actinides) applied. For the heaviest elements they are predictions, since those atoms exist for only fractions of a second.

    Does it run on a phone?

    Yes. On small screens the panels become a bottom sheet, and the cloud density is lowered by default so it stays smooth. You can raise it in the Cloud & Zoom controls.

    Can I share a link to one element?

    Yes. Add the symbol to the address: simulation.html#el=Fe opens the simulator on iron, and the Nucleus Explorer accepts the same form.

    Is it free?

    Yes. It runs in the browser with nothing to install.
